Use these verified 1920 figures as reference points. When introducing a new wage, price, or cost, scale it from the nearest anchor rather than inventing a number from scratch.

| Category | Example | 1920 value |
|---|---|---|
| Wage | Industrial laborer | $20–$28/week |
| Wage | Skilled tradesman (carpenter, machinist) | $30–$40/week |
| Wage | Police officer | $1,400–$1,800/year |
| Goods | Loaf of bread | 8–10¢ |
| Goods | Dozen eggs | ~47¢ |
| Goods | Men's suit | $25–$40 |
| Goods | Pair of shoes | $5–$8 |
| Housing | NYC apartment rent | ~$60/month |
| Housing | Modest house (purchase) | $2,000–$6,300 |
| Vehicle | Ford Model T | ~$440 |
| Vehicle | Average new car | ~$2,500 |

**Scaling method:** when pricing something new, find the closest anchor by category (wage, everyday good, housing, vehicle) and location tier, then adjust proportionally for quality, rarity, and setting (a rural town should run cheaper than NYC; luxury goods scale up from the suit/car anchors, not from bread).

## Crime proceeds

Crime proceeds should track the target's actual cash on hand, not a flat table. Use these bands as sanity checks:

| Target | Typical take |
|---|---|
| Pickpocketing / petty theft | $1–$10 |
| Small store till | $20–$150 |
| Speakeasy or small business safe | $200–$1,000 |
| Bank teller drawer (not vault) | $500–$3,000 |
| Factory payroll robbery | $2,000–$15,000 |
| Bank vault heist | $10,000–$50,000 |

**Reference point:** $10,000 is roughly three years of average household income ($3,269/year) and should read as an exceptional, career-defining score — not a routine job. Scores above $20,000 should carry narrative weight (headlines, heat, retaliation) proportional to their rarity.
